Boss Blues Driver: BD-2 or BD-2W Waza Craft?

Quick verdict: Skip the standard BD-2 and go straight for the BD-2W Waza Craft (about $187) — but only if you plan to actually use Custom mode. If you'd run it as a bright boost into an already-cranked amp (the pedal's original, most common use case), the standard ~$110 BD-2 gets you basically the same result for less.

This Isn't a Vintage-vs-New Story

Unlike the DS-1 or RAT, the Blues Driver hasn't gone through multiple circuit eras — it's been in continuous production since 1995 with a broadly consistent design. So the real decision here isn't "old vs. new," it's standard BD-2 vs. the BD-2W Waza Craft, which is a current, still-manufactured alternative rather than a vintage-market question. That makes this an easier and lower-risk decision than most pedals on this list — no chip lottery, no condition gambling, no auction-market weirdness.

What Custom Mode Actually Is

The Waza's Custom mode isn't a vague "boutique upgrade" — it's specifically built to fix the BD-2's one well-known weakness: thinning out at higher gain settings. That fix has a name in the pedal community: the "Keeley Phat Mod," a well-known aftermarket modification originally done by boutique builder Robert Keeley to add low-end body back into the circuit at high gain. Boss essentially built that fix into the Waza Craft version as a switchable second voice, rather than requiring a third-party mod job on a standard BD-2.

The Part Most Reviews Don't Tell You: Standard Mode Isn't Much of an Upgrade

Here's the honest catch, and it matters for your buying decision: several detailed side-by-side comparisons have found that the Waza's Standard mode sounds nearly identical to a stock BD-2 — close enough that if you're paying the extra ~$77 specifically to get "Standard mode," you're not getting meaningfully more pedal. The entire reason to spend more on the Waza is Custom mode. If you have no interest in ever using it, the price difference buys you very little.

Why This Pedal Splits Opinion by Use Case, Not by Era

The Blues Driver's love/hate reputation isn't about vintage vs. modern units — it's about how you use it. Run as a standalone drive on a clean amp, some players find it thin, especially pushed hard. Run as a bright boost into an already-overdriven or cranked tube amp — which is closer to its intended design and how most of its famous users (including John Mayer) actually run it — it does exactly what it's known for: touch-responsive, amp-like gain that cleans up with your guitar's volume knob. That's the same split seen with the DS-1, but here it maps to a use-case decision you make at the amp, not a hunt for the "right" era of pedal.

Who Should Buy the Standard BD-2

Buy the standard BD-2 if: you're using it primarily as a boost into an already-driven amp, where the difference between Standard and Custom mode matters less, or budget is the deciding factor and you want the classic, well-documented Blues Driver sound at the lowest possible cost.

Who Should Buy the BD-2W Waza Craft

Buy the Waza Craft if: you play it as a standalone drive into a clean amp and want the option to fatten up the low end at higher gain — Custom mode solves the specific complaint most players have about the standard BD-2. It's also a reasonable one-pedal solution if you're not sure yet which use case fits your rig, since you get both voicings to compare directly.

Final Recommendation

If you already know you'll run the Blues Driver as a boost into a cranked amp, save the money and get the standard BD-2 — you likely won't hear much difference from the Waza's Standard mode anyway. If you want it as your main gain source into a clean amp, or you just want the flexibility to A/B both voicings on your own rig, the BD-2W Waza Craft is worth the premium specifically for Custom mode.

FAQ

Is the BD-2W Waza Craft just a BD-2 with better parts? Not exactly — its Standard mode is very close to the stock BD-2, but Custom mode is a genuinely different circuit voicing built to address the standard pedal's high-gain thinness, based on the well-known Keeley Phat Mod approach.

Is the standard BD-2 outdated compared to the Waza version? No — it's the same core circuit that's been in continuous production since 1995. It's not a superseded design, just a single-voicing pedal compared to the Waza's two.

Why do some players say the Blues Driver sounds thin? Usually because they're using it as a standalone drive into a clean amp at higher gain settings, which is where the pedal's known weakness shows up. Run as a boost into an already-overdriven amp, that issue is far less noticeable.

Is there a real vintage-vs-new debate with the BD-2, like the DS-1 or RAT? Not really — the circuit has stayed largely consistent since 1995, so there isn't a widely-cited "old chip is better" story here the way there is with those other pedals.

Should I buy used to save money? Used BD-2 units show up cheap and reliably, and since the circuit hasn't changed dramatically over the years, buying used carries much less risk than it does with pedals that went through major circuit revisions.
